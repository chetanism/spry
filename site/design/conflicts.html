The conflict check

The most expensive mistakes are the ones found after the code is written: a story that contradicts a decision made last month, two slices editing the same file, a feature that quietly duplicates another. The conflict check looks for them before any of that happens.

When it runs

On the draft, before it goes ready (a milestone, epic, feature, story or task) or open (a slice). The skills that write those — milestone, epic, feature, story, task and slice-open — run it for you, and run it again if the draft's scope changes. A bug gets one too, looking for duplicates among open bugs.

How it works

  1. Gather.

    spry related <file> lists the candidates, and the agent reads every one:

    • the parent, and its parent;
    • siblings under the same parent;
    • items sharing a glossary term with the draft;
    • full-text hits on the draft's key phrases across the whole plan;
    • the knowledge files your project always checks (always_check — by default security, performance and the decisions index);
    • decisions whose affects names the draft or one of its parents;
    • for a slice: every open slice, and the files its plan lists.
  2. Classify each finding as one of the seven kinds below.
  3. Resolve it — some the agent can settle; some are your call.
  4. Record the result in the document's own Conflict check section.

The seven kinds of finding

KindMeansResolved by
DuplicateAnother item already says thisasks you merge, drop, or tell them apart
OverlapPart of this is already covered elsewhereNarrow one of them, and say which in both
ContradictionThis says the opposite of an item, decision or ruleasks you which one wins
DependencyThis needs another item firstAdded to blocked_by — never a dropped item, or one that waits on this
ScopeThis is outside its parent's in/out scopeasks you move it, or widen the parent
TermA word clashes with the glossaryUse the glossary's word, or add a row
CollisionA slice touches the same files as an open sliceOrder them, or merge them

Why some are asked and some aren't: a dependency or a glossary clash has a mechanical answer. A duplicate, a contradiction or a scope question is a product decision — the agent can lay out the options, but which story wins is not its call.

What gets recorded

## Conflict check

- Checked 2026-10-05 against: F-1, S-1, F-2, glossary, security
- Overlap: S-1 described the "fewer than 3" case → S-1 keeps allowed lending; S-2 has the refusals
- Contradiction: overdue members — allowed in the draft, refused by the paper rule → refused; F-1/R-4 (Asha, 2026-09-30)
  • The first line says when, and against what: Checked <date> against: …
  • One line per finding: <Kind>: <item or file> <what> → <resolution>.
  • → open for anything not yet resolved.
  • No findings → - No conflicts found. under the first line.
It can't be skipped

spry check refuses ready or open without a dated Checked line, or with any finding still marked → open. A reviewer also checks the section is honest — that it names what related lists.

Why write it down?

  • It is evidence the check happened — and against what. "No conflicts" means little if nobody can see what was looked at.
  • The resolutions are decisions. "S-1 narrowed to the allowed case" explains, months later, why S-1 doesn't mention refusals.
  • It travels with the item. Any agent, in any later session, can read why the item looks the way it does.

Collisions between slices

A Collision is the conflict check's most practical finding: two open slices about to edit the same file. Caught at open, it costs a sentence — "SL-2 goes first, SL-3 rebases". Caught at merge, it costs a painful rebase. It is also how spry decides that two slices can safely be built at the same time in separate worktrees: no shared file. Work as a team →
